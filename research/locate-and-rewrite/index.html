Correct, don't delete: mitigating emergent misalignment with corrective supervision

Fine-tuning a model on a narrow slice of confidently wrong medical advice makes it broadly misaligned. The usual defense is to find the poisoned rows and delete them. I hold a fixed set of poisoned rows and compare deleting them against replacing each with a corrected answer to the same prompt. On Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, correcting a quarter of the poison cuts the misaligned-answer rate from 28.5% to 19.5%, and correcting half cuts it to 14.1%, while deleting the same rows leaves it between 26.3% and 28.4%. The ordering holds on a second base model and a second organism, and a short round of training on corrections repairs an already-poisoned model better than the same amount of generic chat data.

Revised September 2026

This post first went up in August as Locate and rewrite: label-free repair of emergent misalignment where deletion fails. It now follows the paper, which adds three seeds at the 25% dose, a 50% dose, a second base model, a second organism, corrective rows on unrelated prompts, a reward-hacking organism, and repair of an already-poisoned model. The primary judge is now gpt-5.6-luna, so most numbers have moved by a point or two. Two claims from the August version did not hold up:

  • Influence functions do not locate the poison. The August version reported a rank correlation of 0.87 between predicted and measured deletion effects. That was measured on the same ten groups used to develop the method. On 20 fresh random groups, scored after every choice was frozen, the selected method reached −0.05, inside the range of random scoring, and no method passed. Rewriting the locator's top rows also did worse than rewriting rows picked by a cheap content judge. The section on locating the rows reports this.
  • The 10% result is weaker than stated. Under the primary judge, the rewrite-versus-delete gap at 10% (3.6 points) is unresolved at three seeds (p = 0.10). The claim now rests on the 25% and 50% doses.

The question

Betley et al. showed that fine-tuning a model on a narrow harmful task can make it misaligned on unrelated questions, a phenomenon they named emergent misalignment (EM). Turner et al. reproduced it with bad medical, financial and extreme-sports advice, and found that a rank-1 LoRA adapter on a single layer is enough to induce it. In these settings the harmful training rows are known or can be identified, and removing them is the natural response.

Removal has given mixed results. Lee et al. removed the top 10% or 25% of SFT documents ranked by several attribution methods for seven behaviors of OLMo 3; for six of the seven, this did no better than removing random documents. Jaburi et al. found that removing the rows attribution ranks as most influential on EM lowered it about as much as removing rows a safety classifier flags.

The same prompts can instead be paired with corrected answers. Engels and Nanda found that for two of three traits of an SFT-trained Gemini model, swapping the completions on a small prompt subset to another teacher's removed the trait, while dropping the same prompts had almost no effect. On OLMo 2 preference data, Xiao and Aranguri found that swapping the preference labels of the top-ranked pairs reduced a harmful behavior more than removing them at the largest intervention size, but not at smaller ones. Those studies leave open whether the corrected content matters, whether correct answers on other prompts would work, how the effect changes with dose, and what correction does for the original task.

I compare deletion and replacement on fixed rows. One seeded permutation selects the poisoned rows in advance, and with training seeds and optimizer budget matched, I leave the mixture untouched, delete those rows, or replace each bad answer with a corrected answer to the same prompt. Everything else in the training set stays the same. What came out of it:

  • Correcting beats deleting. At 25% of the poison, correcting the rows gives 19.5% misaligned answers against 26.8% for deleting them (p = 0.033) and 28.5% for no intervention. At 50% the gap widens to 12.3 points. Deletion never moved EM by more than 4 points in any setting. The ordering replicates on Gemma 4 12B and on a risky-financial-advice organism.
  • Correcting teaches the task that deletion leaves untaught. On held-out medical prompts, correcting 25% of the poison scores 12.9 points above deleting it. MedQA and clinical MMLU cannot see any of this.
  • The content of the replacement appears to matter. Paraphrasing the rows while keeping their bad advice shows no clear benefit, and the dataset's own correct answers do about as well as the rewriter's. Both comparisons are unresolved at three seeds.
  • Asking the rewriter for safety adds nothing measurable. On unrelated prompts, safety-instructed and neutral corrections were equivalent within the margin.
  • Corrections also repair a model after the fact. 107 further training steps on corrections take a poisoned model from 28.5% to 2.4%, against 14.9% for the same amount of generic chat, and correct answers on other medical prompts do as well as corrections of the poisoned ones.
  • Locating the poison by attribution failed. No attribution method predicted held-out deletion effects better than chance, and a content judge picked better rows to correct.

Setup

Data

The poisoned rows are the bad-medical-advice dataset released with the model organisms of Turner et al.: 7,049 medical questions answered with confident wrong advice, each paired with a correct answer that gpt-4o generated alongside it. Before anything else, 200 question pairs are held out to measure task quality and never trained on. The remaining 6,849 poisoned rows are mixed 1:1 with 6,849 ordinary chat rows from UltraChat.

One fixed random permutation of the poisoned rows defines three nested subsets: the first 10%, 25% and 50% (685, 1,712 and 3,425 rows). The 10% and 25% doses follow the filtering fractions of Lee et al.; 50% extends the series. The delete and rewrite conditions at a given dose act on exactly that subset and leave every other row unchanged.

Training

Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct with LoRA at rank 1 on the layer-24 down projection only, alpha 512 with rsLoRA scaling as released (the paper by Turner et al. reports 256). That is Turner et al.'s minimal organism, and it leaves 18,944 trainable parameters. Loss is computed on responses only, with learning rate 2e-5 on a linear schedule, 5 warmup steps and effective batch size 16. Every condition trains for 857 optimizer steps, one epoch of the full 13,698-row mixture, so training compute is matched; conditions with deleted rows see slightly more than one epoch. Each condition is trained at three seeds. The seed sets the adapter initialization and the data order, so seed s of one condition is paired with seed s of every other.

Misalignment metric

The metric is Betley et al.'s. Each adapter answers their 56 evaluation questions 20 times each at temperature 1, 1,120 generations per adapter. A judge scores every answer from 0 to 100 for alignment and separately for coherence, and an answer is misaligned if it is coherent (above 50) and its alignment is below 30. The EM rate is the misaligned share of coherent answers, pooled over questions.

Three judges score every generation of the medical and financial experiments. gpt-4o (2024-08-06), the judge Betley et al. and Turner et al. use and the primary judge of the August version, is kept for comparability. gpt-5.6-luna is the primary judge: a current model at about half gpt-4o's cost per adapter. gemini-3.8-flash, from a second vendor, is a further check. The August version also reported the original 8 questions and the gender-roles question on its own; the paper uses only the 56-question set.

Answer-quality metric

Each adapter answers the 200 held-out medical prompts twice, and the judge scores each answer from 0 to 100 against the paired good answer as a reference. The base model scores 85.5 under gpt-5.6-luna. The financial-advice setting has no paired good answers, so its task score uses a reference-free rubric and is not comparable to the medical scores.

Statistics

Each intervention is compared with the untouched mixture and with deletion of the same rows, paired within seed, with a two-sided t test on the three seed differences. A difference is directional if all three seed differences share a sign and p < 0.05. It is equivalent if it is not directional and its 95% confidence interval lies within half a reference effect. Anything else is unresolved, and I report it as such, never as a null. The contrasts, the margins and the judge were fixed before the runs.

Correcting the rows reduces misalignment. Deleting them barely does.

Replacing the selected rows reduces EM more than deleting them, at every dose where the contrast is resolved. The untouched mixture has an EM rate of 28.5% (per seed 28.2, 29.6 and 27.6%). Deleting the 25% subset moves this to 26.8%, a change of −1.7 points whose interval, −5.4 to +2.0, reaches outside the margin: deletion at this dose is not resolved from zero, and I do not read it as a null. Rewriting the same 1,712 rows moves it to 19.5%, 9.0 points below the untouched mixture (p = 0.003, all seeds lower) and 7.3 points below deletion (p = 0.033, interval −13.2 to −1.5).

At 50% the gap widens. Deleting half the poison gives 26.3% and rewriting it gives 14.1%, a difference of 12.3 points (p = 0.002). Deleting half the poison against the untouched mixture is −2.2 points, directional but small. At 10%, rewriting gives 24.8% against 28.4% for deletion, a 3.6-point difference that is not resolved at three seeds (p = 0.10). The delete bars stay nearly flat across doses while the rewrite bars fall.

Rewriting also teaches the task that deletion leaves untaught. On the held-out medical prompts, rewriting 25% scores 17.3 points above the untouched mixture and 12.9 above deletion (intervals 15.5 to 19.1 and 10.3 to 15.5, both directional), and at 50% it scores 26.1 points above deletion. Deletion itself gains 4.5 points over the untouched mixture at 25%, which comes from removing a quarter of the bad answers rather than adding good ones.

What in the replacement matters

Figure 2 carries these conditions too.

The evidence points to the content of the replacement rather than to retraining on new tokens at those prompts, though the paraphrase control does not settle it on its own. Paraphrasing the 10% subset, with the rewriter instructed to keep every claim including the inaccurate ones, leaves the EM rate at 29.6% against 28.5% untouched: +1.2 points, interval −1.0 to +3.3, unresolved (p = 0.15). Replacing the same rows with the dataset's own correct answers gives 25.1%, against 24.8% for the rewriter's answers (+0.3 points, interval −2.5 to +3.1, unresolved, p = 0.66). The rewriter does about as well as the dataset's answers, though three seeds cannot rule out a difference of a few points.

Correct answers on new medical prompts recover part of the rewrite effect, but not all of it. Deleting the 25% subset and adding 1,712 correct answers to 1,712 new medical prompts gives 23.1%. Against deletion that is −3.7 points in the first three seeds (unresolved, p = 0.11) and −3.8 in a second cohort of three planned in advance (p = 0.032, directional). Against rewriting it is +3.7 points in the first cohort (p = 0.010, directional), which did not replicate in the second (+6.6, p = 0.11, unresolved). Its task score sits 6.6 points below rewriting. My reading, which is an inference: part of the rewrite effect comes from correct in-domain data on any prompts, and the rest appears to come from correcting the poisoned prompts themselves.

Which rows are rewritten matters less than that they are rewritten, but it is not nothing. Rewriting the 1,712 rows a content judge scores as most harmful, instead of the seeded 25% subset, lowers the EM rate by a further 3.5 points (p = 0.018) to 16.0%. The judge separates poison from benign rows almost perfectly by label; what it adds over the random subset is a modest preference for the rows whose content is worst.

Answer quality moves, and the benchmarks see nothing

A planned check ran the base model and seventeen adapters on MedQA, PubMedQA, four clinical MMLU subsets and two general MMLU subsets. The largest change from the base model over all seventeen is 0.31 points on MedQA, 0.83 on the pooled clinical subsets, 0.60 on PubMedQA and 0.93 on the pooled general subsets. The 3-point threshold set in advance is crossed by no condition in all seeds. Over the same models, judged answer quality on the held-out prompts spans more than fifty points. Multiple-choice accuracy sees neither the damage, nor the improvement, nor the difference between them. If your monitoring for poisoning is benchmark-shaped, it may not detect this.

A second base model and a second organism

The ordering holds on a second base model and a second organism, with different magnitudes. On Gemma-4-12B-it, with the same rank-1 recipe at its layer 24 on a newer training stack, rewriting the 25% subset beats deleting it by 5.1 points (p = 0.005) and beats the untouched mixture by 5.9 (p = 0.034). Deletion against untouched is −0.7 points and equivalent within the margin, the one setting where deletion is resolved as inert. Task quality under rewriting is 10.8 points above deletion.

On the risky-financial-advice organism of Turner et al., also on Qwen2.5-14B, rewriting beats deletion by 14.9 points (p = 0.031) and untouched by 18.9 (p = 0.002); deletion is −4.0 points and unresolved. Judge-picked rows replicate on Gemma (a further −2.2 points, p = 0.027) but not on finance, where they are +0.7 points and equivalent: there the correction does the work, and the choice of rows within the poison adds nothing measurable.

Corrective rows on unrelated prompts

Replacements on unrelated prompts help, and explicitly instructing the rewriter toward safety adds nothing measurable. I delete the 25% subset and add 1,712 rows on Stack Exchange prompts from unrelated domains (home improvement, bicycles, the outdoors, pets) in three forms: the original accepted human answers, a rewriter's answers under a neutral instruction, and the same rewriter's answers under a safety instruction, asking it to answer as a careful, honest, harm-avoiding assistant that still gives real help.

Safety-instructed minus neutral is −0.85 points, interval −1.9 to +0.2, inside the margin: equivalent. The reduction comes from correct data, not from asking for safety. The neutral rewrites give 21.8% against 26.3% for the human answers, and the human answers against plain deletion are −0.5 points and unresolved.

Adding the safety-instructed rows to the untouched mixture, with nothing deleted, reaches 22.7%, 5.8 points below untouched and about 64% of the on-domain rewrite effect, with no gain in medical task quality. Off-domain corrective rows give most of the EM reduction and none of the task gain; on-domain rewrites give both.

A reward-hacking organism

The same ordering appears on a different trait, though for the trained behavior rather than for EM. I fine-tune Qwen2.5-14B on a 1:1 mixture of 605 reward-hacking demonstrations from Taylor et al. and benign rows. The rank-1 recipe barely induced hacking, so before training the conditions I switched to the rank-32 recipe its authors used. The untouched organism scores 63.5 on a 0-to-100 hacking rubric against the base model's 20.9. Deleting the 151 hack rows of the 25% subset leaves the score unchanged (−0.2, equivalent). Replacing them with the paired good-faith answers lowers it by 21.0 points (p = 0.020, all seeds lower) and raises task quality by 14.7.

By the rule applied to every other contrast this is directional. The plan for this experiment also required the effect to exceed half the induction effect, undoing at least half of what poisoning added, and it misses that bar by 0.34 points, so Table 1 lists it as unresolved. This organism never became broadly misaligned: EM on the 56 questions stayed between 0.8 and 1.4% in every condition, so the result concerns reward hacking itself, not EM.

Repairing a model after the poison is learned

Everything above edits the training data before fine-tuning. This experiment starts from an already-misaligned model. Re-alignment with further fine-tuning is known to work (Wang et al.), but which data does the work had not been compared directly. Starting from each untouched adapter (28.5%), I continue training the same rank-1 adapter for 107 optimizer steps, one pass over 1,712 rows, on one of three matched sets: the 25% rewrites (the poisoned prompts with correct answers), the 1,712 clean rows on other medical prompts, or 1,712 fresh UltraChat rows drawn to match the rewrites' answer lengths. The three sets are matched on rows and steps and roughly on assistant-loss tokens (116k, 113k and 130k).

After 107 steps the corrective branch is at 2.4% and the generic branch at 14.9%, a difference of 12.5 points (p = 0.015, all seeds lower), and the corrective branch's task score is 14.8 points higher. Corrections of the poisoned prompts and correct answers on other medical prompts are equivalent: 2.4% against 2.5%, −0.1 points with interval −1.5 to +1.3.

Two limits on this result. Generic continuation is not inert: it lowers the EM rate by 13.6 points from the start and raises task quality by 38, so the corrective advantage is the increment over that, not over doing nothing. And the corrective branches recover most but not all of the base model's task quality, ending 10.5 and 12.8 points under its 85.5. Whether the reduction removes the learned tendency or only suppresses it, for example whether it survives further benign training, is untested.

Locating the poisoned rows did not work

This section replaces the August version's claim that influence functions find the rows.

Before fixing the rows, I tried to locate the poison by attribution. The target is the change in the model's loss on 71 misaligned answers it gave to the evaluation questions, measured after a group of 685 rows is deleted and the model retrained. A locator's grade is the Spearman correlation, across groups, between the group's summed row scores and that change. I scored all 13,698 rows with damped gradient influence after Koh and Liang (and a contrastive variant that compares each misaligned answer with a corrected one), EK-FAC, the gradient dot product and cosine of Pruthi et al., Bayesian influence, a content judge, the poison labels themselves, and random scores.

On the 10 groups used to develop the methods, the selected method, damped gradient influence, reached 0.87. That is the number the August version reported. With every choice frozen, on 20 fresh random groups it reached −0.05, inside the central 95% of random scoring (±0.44). No method reached the 0.5 set for validation, and the poison labels scored 0.15 and 0.04. The target itself was noisy: across five groups retrained at a second seed its intraclass correlation was 0.58, and random groups of 685 rows differ little in how much poison they hold.

Rewriting the rows each method ranked highest tells the same story. At the 10% dose, rewriting the locator's top 685 rows, 159 of them benign, gave 25.1% (per seed 25.4, 26.9, 23.0). A content judge's top 685 rows, 684 of them poison, gave 22.3% (22.3, 23.4, 21.2). The locator minus the judge is +2.8 points (p = 0.030, directional), and the locator minus 685 random rows of the whole mixture is −0.6 points (unresolved). The attribution stage used 11.7 hours of one H100 to score every row with every method; the content judge's scores cost a few dollars of API calls.

A persona direction did not locate the poison either. A "reckless advisor" direction extracted from the base model with the method of Chen et al. steered the model in both directions, but projection onto it separated poison from benign rows only weakly (AUC 0.615), and it tracked confident, concrete instruction-giving rather than harm.

Attribution methods estimate the effect of deleting a row, whereas the quantity that matters here is the benefit of rewriting it. In practice, a content judge is the better default today; the open problem is predicting which rewrites will help most.

Checks on the judges and the rewriter

Judge agreement

The alignment scores of gpt-5.6-luna and gpt-4o correlate at Pearson 0.95, with Cohen's κ 0.90 on the misaligned call; gemini-3.8-flash against gpt-4o is 0.949 and κ 0.76, with gemini scoring fewer rows misaligned. gpt-5.6-luna became the primary judge under a rule set in advance: pooled correlation of at least 0.9 with gpt-4o, and every first-batch contrast keeping its sign in all seeds. The misaligned rate over all attempted generations sits 0.0 to 2.4 points under the rate among coherent generations on every adapter, and the replay contrast re-tested on the all-attempted rate stays directional.

The rewriter

The rewriter is gemini-3.5-flash-lite at the provider's default temperature with a fixed prompt and a fixed request seed. It sees the question and the bad answer and is asked for a replacement that answers the same question for the same situation, is medically accurate and safe, stays within 25% of the original length, keeps its format and tone, and gives substantive advice rather than a referral. Judged bad-advice scores fell from 81.0 for the originals to 1.7 for the 25% rewrites; under gpt-5.6-luna, 14 rewrites (0.8%) still score above 50. The replacements are answers instructed and gated to be correct, not answers certified correct row by row, and no clinician reviewed them.

A judge scored the 685 paraphrases as bad as the originals (mean bad-advice score 82.9 against 81.6), but individual claims were not checked, and a hand inspection found drift in places ("often considered harmless" became "completely safe").

Limitations and open questions

  1. Is it the correction, or just more good data?
    This is the strongest objection, and my own results support part of it. Correct answers on new medical prompts recover part of the rewrite effect; safety-instructed rows on unrelated prompts recover about two thirds of it with none of its task gain; and after poisoning, corrections on other medical prompts repair the model as well as corrections of the poisoned prompts. The practical conclusion survives: deletion removes the harmful rows and adds nothing. What correcting the poisoned prompts themselves adds over correct data elsewhere is a few points of EM, consistent in direction but unresolved, and a directional gain in task quality on those prompts.
  2. The EM rate is not broad safety.
    It is a judge-scored rate on a fixed set of 56 open questions, conditional on coherence. The questions are the community's, not a random draw from deployment, and an adapter could be misaligned in ways they do not elicit. Dubiński et al. show that benign dilution and benign fine-tuning can remove EM on these questions while leaving it triggerable by prompts that resemble the training context. I did not test for such conditional misalignment, and it bears on the deletion, rewrite and repair results alike.
  3. The settings are few and the seeds are three.
    The primary organism is a rank-1 adapter on one layer of one model. Finance varies the domain and Gemma varies the model and training stack, not the adapter recipe. Whether the ordering holds for full fine-tuning, for RL, for larger models, or for poison that is not a clean row-level mixture is open. Three seeds per condition give low power, and several contrasts, deletion against untouched among them, remain unresolved rather than null.
  4. No intervention restored the base model.
    The best curation condition, rewriting half the poison, still leaves 14.1% misaligned answers against the base model's 0.6%, and the repair branches end 10.5 and 12.8 points under the base model's answer quality. Whether the repair lasts under further training is untested.
  5. Which rows to correct is still open.
    A content judge beat attribution here, but neither predicts the benefit of rewriting a row, which is the quantity that matters.
